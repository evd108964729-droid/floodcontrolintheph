Explainer

The scandal in one page

In 2025, reports of "ghost" and substandard flood control projects pushed the issue into national debate. Public works contracts, budget insertions and contractor ties came under scrutiny.

The Philippines spends heavily on flood control because it faces typhoons, monsoon rains and low-lying river basins. Investigations and news reports alleged that some projects were never built, were poorly built, or were paid for at inflated cost. Lawmakers, public works officials and contractors were named in public hearings, and an independent commission was set up to look into infrastructure irregularities. Cases and investigations continue, so check current news for the latest status.

Why it matters

Floods hit the poor hardest

Weak drainage and failed embankments mean repeated flooding in homes, farms and schools.

Informal settlements and farming towns often sit closest to rivers. When flood walls fail or pumps do not work, families lose belongings, income and sometimes lives. Corruption in these projects is not only a budget issue. It is a safety issue.

How it works

How a project is supposed to be checked

Bidding, inspection and public reporting are the safeguards. The question is whether they worked.

Projects normally pass through budget approval, procurement and bidding, construction, and audit. Critics say gaps at each stage, from thin oversight to cozy contractor relationships, let problems slip through. Reform ideas include open project maps, independent inspection and stronger audit follow-up.

Public response

Citizens ask for receipts

Protests, online reporting tools and student groups pushed for names, records and charges.

Large rallies in 2025 demanded accountability. Citizens also began checking project sites themselves and sharing photos of unfinished or missing structures. Transparency advocates say sustained public attention is what keeps investigations moving.

Solutions

Beyond walls: smarter flood control

Experts urge drainage upgrades, watershed protection and local planning alongside concrete structures.

Mangrove and forest protection, river dredging that is verified, early warning systems and better land-use rules can reduce flooding. Spending should be matched to risk maps and measured by outcomes, not only by the number of projects completed.

How the story unfolded

July 2025

The President's State of the Nation Address calls out faulty flood control projects.

Aug to Sept 2025

Reports of ghost projects and contractor links spread. Congressional and Senate hearings begin.

Sept 2025

An independent commission is created to investigate infrastructure projects, and mass rallies are held.

After

Investigations, filings and policy debates continue. Follow trusted news for updates.

Key words to know

Flood control

Structures and systems that reduce flooding, such as dikes, drainage, pumps and river walls.

Ghost project

A project that was paid for but, according to reports, was never built.

Substandard

Built with poor quality, so it may fail when it is needed most.

Corruption

Misusing public money or power for private gain.

Procurement

The process a government uses to buy goods or hire builders, usually through bidding.

Accountability

Being answerable for your actions, and facing consequences if you broke the rules.

Transparency

Making records open so citizens can check how public money is used.

For teachers

A ready-to-use 45 to 60 minute lesson for Grades 7 to 12 (Araling Panlipunan, Civics, English, Media Literacy). Suggested for ages 12 and up.

Learning goals

  • Explain what flood control is and why it matters in the Philippines.
  • Describe how public projects should be checked.
  • Tell verified facts apart from rumors.

Lesson flow

  1. Hook (5 min): Ask, "Who has been affected by flooding?"
  2. Read (10 min): Students read the posts and timeline.
  3. Vocabulary (10 min): Pairs match the key words to examples.
  4. Discuss (15 min): Use the questions below.
  5. Quiz and exit ticket (10 min): Take the quiz, then write one action a citizen can take.

Discussion questions

  • Why does flood control corruption hurt poor communities the most?
  • Which safeguard (bidding, inspection, audit, public reporting) is the most important? Why?
  • How can you check if a news post about this is reliable?
  • What should happen to someone who is found guilty by a court?

Class activities

  • Barangay project hunt: Find one flood control project near you and list what you can verify.
  • Fact-check relay: Groups rate three claims as verified, unverified or false, citing sources.
  • Letter to an official: Write a respectful letter asking for project records.

Teaching note: Many cases are still under investigation. Remind students that a person is presumed innocent until proven guilty, and keep discussion focused on facts, systems and solutions rather than personal attacks.
